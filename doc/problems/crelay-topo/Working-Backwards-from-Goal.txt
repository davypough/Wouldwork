;;; This is a backward analysis of the crelay-topo problem defined in
;;; probs/problem-crelay-topo.lisp.  It is intended to expose prerequisites and useful
;;; planning phases before we look for an exact forward solution.  It is not itself a
;;; validated action sequence.
;;;
;;; "Required" below follows from the current controls, topology, and technology rules.
;;; "Likely arrangement" identifies a promising implementation that still has to be
;;; incorporated into, and validated as part of, a complete forward solution.


HARD BACKWARD CHAIN

1. Final goal: agent1 must reach location19.

   Agent1 must approach from the location16/location17/location18 area and pass gate9.
   Gate9 is open only while plate6, plate7, and plate8 are all depressed.

   In this movement model an agent standing on a plate must first step down to the ground;
   it cannot step off the plate and cross gate9 in the same MOVE.  Therefore agent1 cannot
   be counted as one of the three final plate occupants.  Three other persistent occupants
   must be on plate6, plate7, and plate8 when agent1 crosses gate9.  Live or ghost agents and
   cargo can depress plates when their recorder-side use is legal.

2. Reach the three final plates through gate8.

   Gate8 must be open while agent1 passes from the location15 side to the
   location16/location17/location18 side.  Receiver1 controls gate8, so receiver1 must be
   active at that time.  Any connector or support arrangement supplying the beam must stay
   intact until the required agents and resources have crossed.

3. Reach location15 through gate7.

   Switch2 must be on to open gate7.  Switch2 is reachable only from location14.

4. Reach switch2 through gate6 and screen1.

   Gate6 opens only while plate4 and plate5, at location13 and location12, are both
   depressed.  Screen1 is co-located with gate6 and passes only an empty-handed agent.
   Thus the two plate occupants must be put in place before an empty-handed agent crosses
   to location14 and toggles switch2.

   Switch2 is an important phase boundary:

     switch2 off: gate5 open,  gate7 closed     (the initial state)
     switch2 on:  gate5 closed, gate7 open

   The agent can toggle switch2 on, return through gate6 and screen1 while plate4 and plate5
   are still occupied, and then recover those occupants for later use.  Removing the first
   occupant closes gate6, but by then the agent is back on the location12/location13 side
   and can proceed through the newly opened gate7.

5. Reach plate4 and plate5 through gate5.

   Gate5 does not initially need to be opened: its inverted switch2 control makes it open
   while switch2 is off.  All resources that must pass gate5 should be moved through before
   switch2 is turned on, unless the plan deliberately returns to switch2 to reverse the
   gates again.

6. Reach the location8/location9/location10 area through gate3.

   Plate2, at location7, controls gate3.  Tray1 starts on plate2, so plate2 is initially
   depressed and gate3 is initially open.  Once tray1 is reclaimed, some occupant must be
   returned to plate2 before later agents and resources can cross.  The earlier sketch's
   reference to gate7 here was a name error: plate2 controls gate3; switch2 controls gate7.

7. Leave the starting area through gate1.

   Plate1, at location2, controls gate1.  No cargo starts on the location1/location2 side,
   so the recorder supplies the initial cooperation.  A complete 15-action first cycle has
   now been checked with VALIDATE-SOLUTION and the recorder-cycle validator:

   * agent1 starts the recorder;
   * agent1* moves to location2 and steps onto plate1;
   * agent1 crosses gates1 and 3, retrieves connector1 from location9, and returns;
   * agent1* steps off plate1 and agent1 puts connector1 on plate1;
   * agent1 crosses gate1, removes tray1 from plate2, and stages it on the ground at
     location5 for the box1 subgoal;
   * agent1 and agent1* return to location1; and
   * agent1* stops the recorder.

   The validated closed checkpoint has connector1 on plate1, gate1 open, tray1 free at
   location5, box1 still at location6, agent1 back at recorder1, and one recorder cycle
   used.  This explicitly frees tray1 rather than relying on recorder cleanup to remove its
   initial plate2 support relation.

   A second START-RECORDER followed by agent1* walking through gate1 to location5 is also
   executable.  However, that second start exposes an unresolved recorder representation
   problem: forking connector1 onto the same fixed plate replaces the live ON fact with the
   ghost ON fact.  The ghost can enter the box area, but connector1's persistence through a
   later cycle ending is not yet established.  Repeated-cycle planning must not assume that
   defect away.


RECEIVER1 AND THE BEAM

Required:

* Transmitter1 and receiver1 have the same red chroma.
* Gate4 lies in the useful transmitter-to-location9 beam corridor.  Plate3 at location10
  must remain depressed to keep gate4 open.  This is part of the main dependency chain,
  not an aside.
* A connector placed directly on the ground at location9 is too low to reach receiver1
  across the two one-unit walls.  Raising the connector is required.

Likely arrangement, checked directly in a constructed state:

* Move box1 to location9.
* Place connector1 on box1, giving the connector a beam height of 2.
* Pair connector1 with transmitter1 and receiver1.
* Keep plate3 depressed so gate4 remains open.

That arrangement colors connector1 red, activates receiver1, and opens gate8 in the
current model.  A held tray can in principle provide a different elevated support, but it
would tie up an agent and create more recorder dependencies; the box is the clearer working
hypothesis.


REPEATER1

No useful role for repeater1 is currently apparent.

With every gate treated as open and movable occluders removed, location9 is the only
authored location at a feasible connector height that can see both repeater1 and receiver1.
At that same elevated location the connector can also see transmitter1 and can connect
directly to receiver1.  Adding repeater1 to the pairing graph therefore adds no reach and
does not avoid opening gate4 or raising the connector.

A genuine repeater route would need another elevated connector at a new, useful location
on the receiver side of the blocking walls.  That would consume more live/ghost resources
than the direct location9 arrangement and has no identified advantage.  Repeater1 should
be treated as an available but probably unused apparatus, not as a backward prerequisite.
As a declared problem object it should remain in the model unless the problem definition
itself is intentionally changed.


BOX1, SWITCH1, BLOWER1, AND GATE2

Box1 starts at the elevated location6.  Gate2 is initially open because switch1 is off.  The
first validated recorder cycle stages tray1 at location5 so that an agent can potentially
stand on it to reach location6, retrieve box1, and bring it down to the ordinary floor area.
That tray-assisted recovery is the next candidate subgoal and has not yet been validated.

Turning switch1 on activates blower1 and closes gate2.  No necessary use for that change or
for the blower has yet emerged from the backward chain.  The safe working assumption is to
recover box1 while gate2 remains open and regard switch1/blower1 as optional until a forward
resource plan demonstrates a use for them.


THE LADDERS

The ladders are not merely unused props.  Together they form a one-way, empty-handed return
route that bypasses the three early controlled gates:

  ladder3: location11 -> location10, bypassing closed gate5
  ladder2: location8  -> location5,  bypassing closed gate3
  ladder1: location3  -> location1,  bypassing closed gate1 and returning to recorder1

The staged mobility model was checked with gates 1, 3, and 5 closed and produced this route:

  location11
    --ladder3--> location10
    --walk------> location8
    --ladder2--> location5
    --walk------> location3
    --ladder1--> location1

Because a screen or ladder passes only an empty-handed agent, cargo must be left in its
intended phase before this return.  This route is likely important for bringing a ghost back
to recorder1 to close a cycle after plate occupants have been reclaimed and the ordinary
gate route has closed.  None of the three ladders should be pruned before a complete forward
solution proves that an alternative recorder-closing route is sufficient.


RESOURCE AND PHASE CHECKLIST FOR A FORWARD PLAN

The following commitments do not all have to exist simultaneously.  A useful forward plan
should explicitly show when each is established, when its gate has been crossed for the last
time, and when its occupant can be reclaimed.

* plate1 occupied while leaving the starting area through gate1;
* tray1 initially on plate2, then another occupant committed there while moving the required
  resources through gate3;
* plate3 occupied while the transmitter beam must cross gate4;
* an elevated connector arrangement maintained while receiver1 must hold gate8 open;
* plate4 and plate5 occupied while reaching and returning from switch2 through gate6;
* switch2 on before using gate7, with the resulting closure of gate5 accounted for;
* three persistent occupants available for plate6, plate7, and plate8 while agent1 crosses
  gate9;
* any ghost that must stop the recorder empty-handed and able to use the ladder return chain
  to location1.

The recorder's live/ghost copies are likely needed not just to open gate1, but to satisfy
the peak number of simultaneous plate, beam-support, and agent-position commitments.  A
precise forward solution should be organized around releasing and reusing those resources
at the phase boundaries above.


LOCATION-PRUNING REVIEW

There is no clearly disposable authored location at present:

* locations1-3 anchor the start, plate1, recorder1, and ladder1;
* locations4-6 anchor blower1, switch1 reach, the gate2 jumps, box1, and the elevated ledge;
* location7 anchors plate2;
* locations8-10 anchor ladder2, the elevated connector site, plate3, and ladder3's return;
* locations11-14 anchor ladder3, plates4/5, the gate6/screen passage, and switch2;
* location15 is the necessary standing region between gate7 and gate8;
* locations16-18 anchor the three final plates;
* location19 is the goal; and
* location20 is blower1's elevated destination and part of the authored gate2 jump geometry.

Locations are optional modelling choices in general, but each current location supplies a
distinct apparatus, cargo, traversal, or phase role.  Removing one now would erase a
plausibly necessary route rather than merely reduce duplicate search branching.
